The 60-second answer · 持ち帰りの掟

What do I do with my trash, right now?

There are no trash cans at the event. None.

You carry out 100% of your own waste — that's the official policy. Bring your own bags and sort as you go: 🔥 burnable 燃えるごみ · 🥫 cans 缶 · 🍾 glass びん · 🧴 PET bottles ペットボトル. Compress, double-bag against smell — palm civets raid camps at night.

No car? The GATE takes your sorted trash on the way out.

Official exception for public-transport attendees only: hand sorted bags to the gate at exodus — bins, cans and PET separated. Fire ash too hot to carry? There's a BBQ grill at the gate that accepts burn residue (燃えかす). Drivers: assume this is not for you — your trash rides home with you.

Never, ever dump at a konbini, station or supermarket.

Not even trash from things you bought there. The event treats this as severe as a law violation — a Japanese camp event once lost its next year over roadside dumping. Store bins are for small on-the-spot purchases by that store's customers, never for camp trash.

Graywater goes home in a bottle.

Only pure water may be poured on site. Soapy, oily, food-touched water — and yes, coffee and tea — go into a closed, labeled container (グレイウォーター) and leave with you. Never into the portable toilets: it kills them for everyone. Tea leaves don't get buried; "biodegradable" is not Leave No Trace.

Smokers: portable ashtray. Mandatory.

携帯灰皿 (keitai haizara) — sold at every konbini for a few hundred yen. Butts and ash go home with you. Glitter, confetti and micro-beads are banned outright; zip-tie clippings and tape shreds go straight into a sealed bag before the wind takes them.

飛ぶ鳥跡を濁さず — "the departing bird does not muddy the water."
Japan had Leave No Trace before we had a name for it.
Where you're standing · 嬬恋村

This village officially refuses your trash

The burn is on a working cattle ranch in Tsumagoi Village, Gunma (嬬恋村) — Japan's #1 summer-cabbage producer, at 1,400 m on the Manza Highway toll road. The village has made a formal declaration:

「廃棄物の持ち込みを認めないむら」
"A village that does not accept waste brought in from outside" — no outside collection, transport, processing or disposal. Official declaration, Tsumagoi Village.

That means a visitor has no legal public disposal option in the village — at all. Local collection stations belong to resident neighborhood groups; using one as an outsider is treated like dumping on someone's lawn, and the village facility only accepts waste from residents and villa owners. Illegal dumping (不法投棄) in mountains, rivers or roadsides is a crime — up to ¥10M / 5 years under the Waste Management Act. The only moves that exist: the gate (if you came by train), your accommodation (modestly, with gratitude), or your own home city's bins by its own rules.

How Japanese sorting works · ごみの分け方

Every town has its own rulebook

The single most important lesson: garbage rules are municipal, not national. "Burnable" in one city is "non-burnable" in the next. Each town sells its own designated bags (指定袋 — at supermarkets, drugstores and konbini in that town; that's how you pay for disposal), publishes its own calendar, and collects at resident-run stations, usually 6–8 a.m. on the right morning only. Wrong bag — or a black bag — simply doesn't get collected. There are essentially no public trash cans anywhere (most were removed after 1995 and never came back); everyone carries their trash home. So do you.

Below is the real system of Komoro City (小諸市, Nagano) — the nearby town across the prefecture line where many burners stay, and one of the only cities in Japan that composts all food waste into fertilizer. Five bags. If you're staying somewhere else, the shape will be similar but the details will differ — ask your host.

Burnable

燃やすごみ · red bag

Paper scraps, dirty plastics, all plastic products (toothbrush, hangers, toys — anything without the プラ mark), leather, shoes, cloth, wetted ash. Everything cut ≤ 50 cm.

Landfill

埋立ごみ · blue bag

Glass, ceramics, metal items, mirrors, umbrellas, bulbs. Knives & broken glass: wrap in newspaper and label 刃物 / ガラス.

Food waste

生ごみ · paper bag

Veggie scraps, leftovers, shells, coffee grounds — drained of water. Komoro turns it into fertilizer.

Plastic packaging

プラ容器包装 · green bag · free

Only items with the プラ mark: trays, wrappers, bags, styrofoam, PET caps & labels — rinsed and dried.

Resources

資源物 · containers · free

Cans → blue container. Bottles → color-sorted. PET → net. Paper & cardboard tied with paper string. Clothes tied crosswise.

Getting out · 脱出

🚌 Exodus — how you get home
(and how your trash gets home with you)

You are at 1,400 m, on a working cattle ranch, beside a cars-only private toll road. Burning Japan's own access page sums the situation up in one line: "only 3 buses a day." That is not a figure of speech. Miss the last one and you are sleeping in Gunma — so the exodus plan is part of your packing list, not an afterthought.

Everything below comes from the Seibu Kanko Bus official autumn 2026 timetable, valid from 1 October 2026 — the very PDF Burning Japan links from its Access page. The ranch has its own bus stop in it: 嬬恋牧場 愛妻の鐘 (Tsumagoi Bokujo · Aisai no Kane), right at the bell. Route letter M.

Why an exodus chapter lives on a waste site

Because how you leave decides where your garbage ends up. The bus doesn't have a luggage bay. The station has a konbini across the road. Those two facts have ended events before.

No car? The GATE takes your sorted trash on the way out.

This is the official concession for public-transport attendees, and it is the single best reason to come by train. Hand it over already separated — 燃えるごみ burnable / 缶 cans / ペットボトル PET, in their own bags. Fire residue (燃えかす) goes on the BBQ grill at the gate if it's too hot to carry. Drivers: this is not for you — 100% of it goes home in your boot.

Sort before you queue. You cannot sort while boarding.

Picture it honestly: one door, a numbered-ticket machine, a fare box, people behind you, a driver on a timetable, 8.8 km of switchbacks ahead. Nobody tips out a bin bag on the step. Do the separation at your camp with daylight and gloves, tie it, compress it, double-bag it against smell, and arrive with bags you can carry on your lap.

Never the station. Never the konbini. Never the bus terminal.

The convenience store beside Manza-Kazawaguchi Station is the most tempting bin in Gunma, and dumping there is absolutely forbidden — Burning Japan's words, no exception even for things you bought in that shop. The same goes for Karuizawa Station, Kusatsu's bus terminal and every rest stop on the way. Tsumagoi Village formally refuses outside waste; a past Japanese burn lost its venue over exactly this. Carry it to where you sleep, then sort it by that town's rules.

Graywater rides home too — cap tight.

Soapy, oily, sweet, tea- or coffee-coloured water goes in sealed bottles labelled グレイウォーター, upright, in a bag that can hold a leak. Two hours of mountain hairpins will test every lid you own. Pure water only may go in the street drains at the burn; nothing goes in the toilets, ever.

Plan the pack-out before you plan the party.

Coming by train means every can, bottle and bag you bring, you also carry back up the step of a bus. That is the real reason burners buy fewer cans, decant drinks into a bottle, strip packaging at home and skip paper plates. Leave No Trace isn't only an ethic out here — it's a weight limit.

  • Timetables are seasonal. Everything above is the autumn diagram, valid from 1 Oct 2026. A winter diagram replaces it (and in these mountains winter diagrams cut service). Re-download the PDF before you travel and check the stop-post on the day.
  • Burning Japan's own Access page says the limited express 草津・四万 No.1/No.3 runs direct from Ueno to Manza-Kazawaguchi with no change at Takasaki. JR East's own express timetable for 1 Oct–30 Nov 2026 lists 長野原草津口 as the end of that line, and the Manza-Kazawaguchi departure board shows only local (普通) trains. Assume you change at Naganohara-Kusatsuguchi and ride a local the last four stops (~19 min). Verify with JR before buying.
  • The Survival Guide's onsen-shuttle times (ranch 13:19 / 15:29) differ by a few minutes from the official autumn PDF (13:14 / 15:29) — the guide itself warns those are last year's. Trust the PDF.
  • Whether the last train (20:14) still reaches Tokyo the same night. We could not confirm a same-night arrival off that departure; the last connection we could verify leaves at 18:59.
  • Taxis are not on standby. There is no rank at the ranch, and Burning Japan notes plainly that Uber, GO and DiDi barely work outside major cities and do not work in Tsumagoi. You must telephone, in advance, in Japanese.
  • Whether the gate still accepts trash after 18:00 on LNT Day, or at all on DBK Day — not stated anywhere official. Assume no and plan to carry it.
  • Luggage limits on the bus. No published policy found. It's a single-door rural service bus with no hold — take it as "what you can hold" until someone tells you otherwise.
  • Walking is not an option. It is 8.8 km from the bell to the station, which sounds walkable — but the Manza Highway is a cars-only private toll road, and "do not walk outside the shoulder line" is one of Burning Japan's five absolute rules, because an accident there can end the event on the spot. Don't.

No ride at all? Burning Japan runs its rideshare board on its own Discord — the carpool channel is the community's actual answer to "I have no car," both directions, and it's where last-minute exodus seats appear. Ask early, offer to chip in for tolls and petrol, and tell your driver how many bags you're bringing before they say yes. The toll road alone costs money; a quiet ¥2,000 and a cold drink is the gift economy working properly.

Hot springs, while you still can: Manza Onsen is ~15 min by car, Kusatsu ~30 min, roughly ¥800–1,000, and Manza's day-visitor desks stop taking people around 16:00 — so the 10:00–14:00 window is the one. Manza is reported to be relatively tattoo-tolerant. Go clean, leave sorted, catch the 16:53.

  • Bus and train times change with the season, and one official page contradicts another about the limited express. Those gaps have their own honest box in the exodus chapter — read it before you plan your ride home.
